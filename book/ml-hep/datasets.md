Machine-learning methods in particle physics are compared on shared
datasets. A public dataset fixes the inputs, the labels and usually the split
into training and test samples, so that two models trained on it can be
compared on equal terms, and it gives researchers outside the experimental
collaborations realistic collisions to work with. This chapter collects the
datasets most used for training and benchmarking, grouped by task, and gives
for each the paper that introduced it and the record that holds its files.
The entries were last checked against their records in November 2026.

## Citing a dataset

A dataset in this field is described by two documents, and a result obtained
on it cites both.

!!! definition "Reference paper"
    The publication that introduced a dataset. It states how the events were
    generated and simulated, defines the task the dataset was released for,
    and usually reports the first results on it.

!!! definition "Data record"
    The archived copy of a dataset's files, with a persistent identifier,
    normally a DOI, issued by the repository that holds it: Zenodo, the CERN
    Open Data Portal, Hugging Face or a similar service.

The paper credits the design of the benchmark; the record identifies the
exact files a result was obtained on. Most records ask to be cited together
with their paper, and the tables in this chapter give both, paper first, in
their last column.

Records change after they are published. Zenodo issues a DOI for every
version of a record and a *concept DOI* that always resolves to the newest
one: version 0 of the Top Tagging Reference Dataset is
10.5281/zenodo.2603256, and its concept DOI is 10.5281/zenodo.2603255
[@toptag-data]. A result cites the version it used. The CERN Open Data Portal
publishes a revised dataset as a new record instead: the ATLAS top-tagging
data released in 2022 [@atlas-toptag-2022-data] were superseded in 2024 by a
record that adds systematic variations [@atlas-toptag-data].

The tables also state how each dataset was simulated, because that decides
what a result on it can claim. Four levels of detector description occur,
listed in Table 1.1.

| Level | Detector description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Generator level | None: the stable particles of the event generator, sometimes clustered into jets | Quark and gluon jets |
| Parametrised simulation | Delphes, which applies efficiency and resolution functions to the stable particles | JetClass, LHC Olympics |
| Full simulation | Geant4 transport through a detector model, followed by the experiment's reconstruction | ATLAS top tagging, CaloChallenge |
| Collision data | Recorded events, reconstructed by the experiment | Aspen Open Jets |

Table: The levels of detector description in public datasets, from the least
to the most realistic.

!!! remark "What a parametrised detector leaves out"
    A model trained on Delphes output has learnt a detector made of
    efficiency and smearing functions. Effects that arise from the material,
    such as photon conversions and nuclear interactions in the tracker, are
    absent, and pile-up is present only where it was overlaid on purpose. A
    gain measured on such a dataset is evidence about the architecture;
    whether it holds for a real detector is shown only on full simulation or
    on data.

## Jet tagging

Jet tagging, the classification of a jet by the particle that initiated it,
has more public datasets than any other task, and a new architecture is
usually tested on it first. Table 1.2 lists the datasets described below.

The **Top Tagging Reference Dataset** [@toptag-data] was produced for the
Lorentz-layer tagger of [@butter2018] and became the common benchmark of the
comparison of top taggers in [@kasieczka2019]. Its signal is hadronically
decaying top quarks and its background QCD dijets, generated with Pythia 8 at
$\sqrt{s} = 14$ TeV without multiple parton interactions or pile-up, and
passed through Delphes with its ATLAS card. Jets are clustered from the
Delphes particle-flow objects with the anti-$k_T$ algorithm and $R = 0.8$,
in the range $550 < p_T < 650$ GeV and $|\eta| < 2$, and each top jet is
matched to a top quark and to all of its decay partons within
$\Delta R = 0.8$. The record stores the four-momenta of the 200 leading
constituents of each jet, split into 1.2 million training, 400,000
validation and 400,000 test jets.

!!! remark "A saturated benchmark"
    The best taggers in the comparison of [@kasieczka2019] already reach an
    area under the ROC curve of about 0.98 on this dataset, so the
    differences between newer architectures on it are small.

The **quark and gluon jets** of [@komiske2019] are generated without any
detector simulation [@qg-pythia-data]. Quark jets come from
$qg \to Z(\to \nu\bar\nu)q$ and gluon jets from
$q\bar q \to Z(\to \nu\bar\nu)g$ in Pythia 8 at 14 TeV, clustered with
anti-$k_T$ and $R = 0.4$ in the range $500 < p_T < 550$ GeV and
$|y| < 1.7$. The record holds two versions, one without charm and bottom
quarks in the hard process, which is the one used in the paper, and one with
them; each is 20 files of 100,000 jets, half quarks and half gluons. A
companion record generated with Herwig 7.1.4 [@qg-herwig-data] allows the
dependence on the parton shower and hadronisation model to be studied.

**JetClass** [@qu2022; @jetclass-data] was released with the Particle
Transformer and is about two orders of magnitude larger than the public jet
datasets before it: 100 million jets for training, 5 million for validation
and 20 million for testing. Its ten classes are quark and gluon jets as
background and jets from $H \to b\bar b$, $H \to c\bar c$, $H \to gg$,
$H \to 4q$, $H \to \ell\nu qq'$, $Z \to q\bar q$, $W \to qq'$,
$t \to bqq'$ and $t \to b\ell\nu$. The hard processes are generated with
MadGraph5_aMC@NLO and showered with Pythia, with multiple parton interactions
but without pile-up, and the detector is simulated with Delphes using its CMS
card, with the impact parameters of charged particles smeared to the
resolution of the CMS tracker. Jets are clustered with anti-$k_T$ and
$R = 0.8$ in the range $500 < p_T < 1000$ GeV and $|\eta| < 2$. Its
successor **JetClass-II** [@li2024; @jetclass2-data] was made to pre-train
the Sophon model for resonance searches. Its 188 classes include resonant
jets with two, three or four prongs initiated by quarks, gluons or leptons,
over a wider range of masses, and it follows the JetClass simulation with an
average pile-up of 50 added and then removed by per-particle pile-up
identification.

The **hls4ml jet dataset** [@duarte2018; @hls4ml-data] was prepared for
studies of neural-network inference on FPGAs. It contains light-quark, gluon,
$W$, $Z$ and top jets with transverse momenta near 1 TeV, generated at
13 TeV with MadGraph5_aMC@NLO and Pythia 8, and provides high-level jet
features [@duarte2018] together with jet images and lists of up to 150
constituents [@moreno2020jedi].

The **ATLAS top-tagging data** [@atlas2024; @atlas-toptag-data] bring the
same task to the full simulation of an LHC detector. The nominal sample has
92.8 million training and 10.3 million test jets, with equal numbers of top
jets and light-quark or gluon jets. Further samples repeat the evaluation with
one systematic effect varied at a time: the energy scale, resolution and
position of calorimeter clusters; the efficiency, fake rate and bias of
tracks; top jets from Pythia and from Herwig; and background jets with
cluster and with string hadronisation. They allow the systematic uncertainty
of a tagger to be quoted beside its performance, which is the subject of the
reference paper. The 2022 record [@atlas-toptag-2022-data], with 42 million
training and 2.5 million test jets, is superseded.

!!! warning "Training weights"
    Every jet in the ATLAS samples carries a training weight, and the record
    requires that each jet's contribution to the loss be multiplied by it. A
    tagger trained without the weights cannot be compared with the published
    results.

The **CMS $H \to b\bar b$ sample** [@moreno2020hbb; @cms-hbb-data] is full CMS
simulation of 13 TeV collisions generated with Pythia 8, released to develop
taggers that separate jets from $H \to b\bar b$ decays from QCD jets. Jets are
clustered from particle-flow candidates with anti-$k_T$ and $R = 0.8$, with
pile-up mitigated by charged-hadron subtraction, and each carries the
features of its charged tracks and of the secondary vertices within
$\Delta R < 0.8$ of its axis.

Two smaller datasets serve narrower tasks. The **jet images** of
[@deoliveira2017] are 872,666 images of $25 \times 25$ pixels of $W$ boson
and QCD jets from Pythia 8.219 at 14 TeV [@jetimages-data]. The jets are
clustered with anti-$k_T$ and $R = 1.0$, selected in
$250 < p_T < 300$ GeV and $60 < m < 100$ GeV, deposited on a grid of
$0.1 \times 0.1$ in $\eta$ and $\phi$ that stands in for a calorimeter, and
preprocessed as in [@deoliveira2016]. The **secondary-vertex dataset** of
[@shlomi2021] contains jets from $t\bar t$ events at 14 TeV, simulated with
Pythia 8 and an ATLAS-like Delphes detector, with the tracks of each jet
described by their perigee parameters and covariance; the task is to
partition the tracks of a jet by their vertex of origin [@sv-data].

### Jets from recorded collisions

Two datasets bring recorded collisions into the same form. **MOD**
[@komiske2020; @mod-data] reprocesses the CMS 2011A Jet Primary Dataset,
recorded at 7 TeV, into HDF5 files of the jets above $p_T = 375$ GeV with
their particle-flow constituents, and is read through the `energyflow`
package. **Aspen Open Jets** [@amram2025; @aoj-data] provides about 178
million jets from the CMS 2016 JetHT open data, which CMS released in its
MINIAOD format, and is described by its authors as the largest ML-ready
dataset for LHC applications. It was made for pre-training foundation
models: pre-training OmniJet-α on it improves the generation of the
simulated top and QCD jets of JetClass.

| Dataset | Simulation | Contents | Cite |
|---|---|---|---|
| Top Tagging Reference Dataset | Pythia 8, Delphes (ATLAS) | 2 M jets; top vs QCD | [@kasieczka2019; @toptag-data] |

## Generative models and fast simulation

Generative models are trained to reproduce a distribution rather than a
label. Their datasets come in two kinds, particle clouds of jets and
calorimeter showers for fast detector simulation, listed in Table 1.3.

**JetNet** [@kansal2021; @jetnet-data] was introduced with the message-passing
generative adversarial network and holds gluon, light-quark, top, $W$ and $Z$
jets with transverse momenta near 1 TeV, produced in 13 TeV collisions and a
simplified detector, between 170,000 and 178,000 per class. Each jet is a list of up to 30 constituents, each described by its
pseudorapidity, azimuth and transverse momentum relative to the jet and by a
mask; **JetNet150** [@jetnet150-data] keeps up to 150. The `jetnet` package
[@kansal2023] loads both and implements the metrics used to evaluate
generated jets, among them the Fréchet and kernel physics distances.

The **Fast Calorimeter Simulation Challenge 2022**, or CaloChallenge
[@krause2025], compared 31 generative models of calorimeter showers on three
datasets of increasing granularity. Dataset 1 [@calo-ds1] is drawn from the
ATLAS Geant4 open datasets: voxelised showers of photons and charged pions
entering the calorimeter at $0.2 < \eta < 0.25$, at 15 incident energies from
256 MeV to 4 TeV in powers of two. Datasets 2 and 3 [@calo-ds2; @calo-ds3]
are Geant4 showers of electrons with energies drawn log-uniformly between
1 GeV and 1 TeV, in a cylindrical calorimeter of 45 layers of silicon and
tungsten. Dataset 2 divides each layer into 9 radial and 16 angular cells,
6,480 voxels in all; Dataset 3, produced with the Par04 example of Geant4,
into 18 and 50, 40,500 voxels in all. Each dataset has files for training and
separate files for evaluation.

The **CaloGAN** showers [@paganini2018; @calogan-data] are photons, positrons
and charged pions in a longitudinally segmented electromagnetic calorimeter,
released with the CaloGAN generative adversarial network.

| Dataset | Simulation | Contents | Cite |
|---|---|---|---|
| JetNet, JetNet150 | Simplified detector | 5 classes; up to 30 or 150 particles | [@kansal2021; @jetnet-data; @jetnet150-data] |
| CaloChallenge Dataset 1 | Full ATLAS simulation | Photon and pion showers | [@krause2025; @calo-ds1] |
| CaloChallenge Datasets 2 and 3 | Geant4 | Electron showers; 6,480 or 40,500 voxels | [@krause2025; @calo-ds2; @calo-ds3] |
| CaloGAN | Geant4 | Photon, positron and pion showers | [@paganini2018; @calogan-data] |

Table: Public datasets for generative models of jets and of calorimeter
showers.

## Anomaly detection

Anomaly detection asks a model to find events unlike the Standard Model
background without being told what the signal is. Its datasets therefore
separate a labelled development sample from blinded samples on which the
methods are judged (Table 1.4).

The **LHC Olympics 2020** [@kasieczka2021] posed the problem as a resonance
search in dijet events. Its R&D dataset [@lhco-rnd-data] contains 1 million
QCD dijet events and 100,000 signal events $W' \to XY$ with $X \to jj$ and
$Y \to jj$, for masses of 3.5 TeV, 500 GeV and 100 GeV, generated with
Pythia 8 and Delphes 3.4.1 without pile-up or multiple parton interactions,
and selected by a single large-radius jet ($R = 1$) with $p_T > 1.3$ TeV.
Every event lists its reconstructed particles as massless $(p_T, \eta, \phi)$
triplets, together with a truth bit for signal. The challenge itself used
three black boxes of 1 million events each and a background sample of the
same size [@lhco-bb-data]; the solutions for black boxes 1 and 3 were added
to the record after the challenge.

!!! warning "Different trigger thresholds"
    The R&D dataset requires a jet with $p_T > 1.3$ TeV, the black boxes and
    their background sample a jet with $p_T > 1.2$ TeV. A result obtained on
    one selection does not carry over to the other unchanged.

The **Dark Machines** challenge [@aarrestad2022] provides more than a billion
simulated Standard Model events, equivalent to 10 fb$^{-1}$ of 13 TeV
collisions, in four analysis channels, with more than 40 signal samples. The
events are generated with MadGraph5_aMC@NLO and Pythia 8 and passed through
Delphes with its ATLAS card. Each is given as a list of reconstructed objects
with their four-momenta and the missing transverse momentum, in CSV files on
Zenodo: the full generation [@dm-gen-data], a labelled sample prepared for a
hackathon [@dm-hackathon-data], and an unlabelled secret dataset
[@dm-secret-data] that stays blinded, so that future methods can be compared
without bias.

The **ADC2021** dataset [@govorkova2022] targets anomaly detection in the
first level of the trigger, where every collision at the 40 MHz rate of the
LHC must be judged in real time. Its training sample [@adc-train-data] is a
mixture of Standard Model processes at 13 TeV, simulated with Delphes using
its CMS Phase-2 card and filtered by requiring an electron or muon with
$p_T > 23$ GeV, a filter chosen to keep the simulation tractable. The signal samples and a blinded test set are listed on the
challenge page, <https://mpp-hep.github.io/ADC2021/>.

| Dataset | Simulation | Contents | Cite |
|---|---|---|---|
| LHC Olympics 2020 | Pythia 8, Delphes | 1.1 M events (R&D); 3 black boxes of 1 M | [@kasieczka2021; @lhco-rnd-data; @lhco-bb-data] |
| Dark Machines | MadGraph5_aMC@NLO, Pythia 8, Delphes (ATLAS) | Over 10$^9$ events; 4 channels | [@aarrestad2022; @dm-gen-data; @dm-hackathon-data; @dm-secret-data] |
| ADC2021 | Delphes (CMS Phase-2), lepton filter | Standard Model mixture and signals | [@govorkova2022; @adc-train-data] |

## Event classification and inference

Datasets of whole events serve two purposes: classifying events as signal or
background, and inferring a physics parameter from a sample of them
(Table 1.5).

The **HIGGS** and **SUSY** datasets of [@baldi2014] showed that deep
networks given only low-level kinematics can outperform shallow networks
given features designed by physicists. Both were simulated for 8 TeV
collisions with MadGraph 5, Pythia and Delphes. HIGGS [@higgs-uci] contains
11 million events of a heavy-Higgs signal in the $\ell\nu jjb\bar b$ final
state and of top-pair background, each described by 21 low-level features
and 7 invariant masses built from them; SUSY [@susy-uci] contains 5 million
events with 8 low-level and 10 derived features. **HEPMASS**
[@baldi2016; @hepmass-uci], with 10.5 million events simulated in the same
way, was made for classifiers that take the mass of the hypothesised
particle as an input. Through the UCI Machine Learning Repository, all three are also used
outside physics as tabular benchmarks.

The **HiggsML** challenge of 2014 [@adambourdarios2015; @higgsml-data]
released 818,238 events of official ATLAS full simulation: $H \to \tau\tau$
signal with $m_H = 125$ GeV against $Z \to \tau\tau$, $t\bar t$ and $W$
backgrounds, in events with an electron or muon and a hadronically decaying
tau. Each event carries a weight normalised to the 2012 data, and submissions
were ranked by the approximate median significance of the events they
selected. The challenge ran on Kaggle from May to September 2014.

The **FAIR Universe HiggsML Uncertainty** dataset [@benato2025; @fair-data]
returns to $H \to \tau\tau$ with a different task: participants report a
confidence interval for the signal strength $\mu$ in the presence of
systematic uncertainties, which the organisers vary through nuisance
parameters such as detector calibrations and background normalisations. It
contains 280 million events with 28 features, simulated with Pythia 8.2 and
Delphes 3.5.0 and weighted to correspond to two weeks of LHC data taking. The
competition was held at NeurIPS 2024.

The **OmniFold** datasets [@andreassen2020; @omnifold-data] were released for
machine-learned unfolding. They contain $Z$+jet events at 14 TeV with a $Z$
transverse momentum above 150 GeV, generated with Herwig 7.1.5 and with
Pythia 8.243 in the ATLAS A14 tune and two of its variations, and passed
through Delphes 3.4.2 with its CMS card. Anti-$k_T$ jets with $R = 0.4$ are
given at both particle level and detector level, so that one generator can
play the part of data and another that of simulation.

| Dataset | Simulation | Contents | Cite |
|---|---|---|---|
| HIGGS, SUSY | MadGraph 5, Pythia, Delphes | 11 M and 5 M events; 28 and 18 features | [@baldi2014; @higgs-uci; @susy-uci] |
| HEPMASS | MadGraph 5, Pythia, Delphes (ATLAS) | 10.5 M events; signal at several masses | [@baldi2016; @hepmass-uci] |
| HiggsML 2014 | Full ATLAS simulation | 0.82 M weighted events; $H \to \tau\tau$ | [@adambourdarios2015; @higgsml-data] |
| FAIR Universe HiggsML Uncertainty | Pythia 8.2, Delphes 3.5.0 | 280 M weighted events; systematic variations | [@benato2025; @fair-data] |
| OmniFold $Z$+jets | Herwig 7, Pythia 8, Delphes (CMS) | Particle- and detector-level jets | [@andreassen2020; @omnifold-data] |

## Reconstruction

Reconstruction datasets provide detector-level inputs, such as hits, tracks
and calorimeter deposits, together with the truth that a reconstruction
algorithm should recover (Table 1.6).

The **TrackML** challenge [@amrouche2020; @amrouche2023] posed track finding
at the pile-up of the High-Luminosity LHC. Each event is a $t\bar t$
interaction generated with Pythia 8 with 200 soft QCD interactions overlaid,
propagated through a fast simulation of a generic silicon tracker. The
dataset holds about ten thousand events, a billion hits and a hundred million
tracks, with the assignment of hits to particles as truth. The accuracy phase
ran on Kaggle in 2018 [@trackml-data], and the throughput phase that followed
scored speed as well as accuracy.

**ColliderML** [@elitez2025; @colliderml-data] is a full simulation of the
OpenDataDetector, a detector model not tied to any experiment, under the
same conditions of 14 TeV collisions and an average pile-up of 200. Its first
release contains about a million events of ten Standard Model and
beyond-Standard-Model processes, together with samples of single particles.

The **MLPF** datasets give detector inputs together with the generator
particles that a particle-flow network should reconstruct. The first
[@pata2021; @mlpf-delphes-data] contains 50,000 $t\bar t$ and 5,000 QCD
events at 14 TeV with an average pile-up of 200, simulated with Pythia 8 and
a CMS-like Delphes detector. The second [@pata2024; @clic-data] is a full
simulation of the CLIC detector in Key4hep of $e^+e^-$ collisions at
380 GeV, namely $t\bar t$, $Z^* \to q\bar q$, $ZH$ with $H \to \tau\tau$
and hadronic $WW$, stored in the EDM4hep format with the tracks, calorimeter
hits and Pandora particle-flow candidates.

Two datasets come from neutrino experiments. **PILArNet**
[@adams2020; @pilarnet-data] provides simulated images of particle
interactions in liquid-argon time projection chambers, in two and three
dimensions at resolutions of 192, 512 and 768 pixels, with a semantic label
for each energy deposit and the grouping of deposits into particles, for
segmentation and clustering. The **IceCube – Neutrinos in Deep Ice**
competition [@eller2023; @icecube-data] asked participants to reconstruct
the direction of neutrinos from the pulses recorded by the sensors of
IceCube, with nearly 140 million simulated events of all flavours between
100 GeV and 100 PeV for training; the three best solutions are described in
[@bukhari2024].

|---|---|---|---|
| TrackML | Pythia 8, fast tracker simulation | About 10$^4$ events; hits and truth tracks | [@amrouche2020; @amrouche2023; @trackml-data] |
| ColliderML | Full simulation (OpenDataDetector) | About 10$^6$ events; 10 processes | [@elitez2025; @colliderml-data] |

!!! summary "Key points"
    - A result cites both the reference paper of a dataset and its data
      record, and names the version of the record it used.
    - The level of detector simulation decides what a result can claim:
      generator-level and Delphes datasets compare architectures, while full
      simulation and recorded data test whether a gain holds for a real
      detector.
    - In jet tagging the Top Tagging Reference Dataset and the quark and
      gluon jets are the conventional baselines, JetClass is the large-scale
      benchmark, and the ATLAS top-tagging data add full simulation and
      systematic variations.
    - Anomaly-detection datasets separate labelled development samples from
      blinded challenge samples, whose selections can differ.
    - Fast calorimeter simulation is compared on the three CaloChallenge
      datasets, which range from a few hundred to 40,500 voxels per shower.
